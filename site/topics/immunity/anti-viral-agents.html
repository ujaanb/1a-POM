4.10 Anti-Viral Agents

Complete notes from the lecture PDF (every Q&A block). 53 revision prompts.

Overview

How does the Zika virus infect its victims and what complications are caused?

  • A mosquito (vector) carries the virus and bites a woman during pregnancy
  • This affects the brain development of the baby and leads to microcephaly (abnormal smallness of the head)

What is the best definition of a virus?

An infectious obligate intracellular parasite.

How does a virus encode its genetic material?

Nucleic acid genome (either RNA or DNA)

What is a virion? Which is the only microscope that can be used to see viruses?

  • A viral particle.
  • Electron microscope.

What is the difference between an enveloped and non-enveloped virus?

  • An enveloped virus has an outer membrane derived from the host cell membrane
  • A non-enveloped virus has no outer membrane and it consists of purely protein.

What is the difference between a pleiomorphic and typical shaped enveloped virus?

  • A pleiomorphic virus has many shapes it can assume
  • A typical shaped virus can only assume a particular shape - making it easy to recognise.

What is a tegument?

A bunch of proteins incorporated inside the outer envelope of a virus.

What special component do viruses with RNA instead of DNA have?

Reverse transcriptase enzymes to help them convert RNA back to DNA.

Why do RNA viruses have a higher mutation rate than DNA viruses?

  • RNA viruses use their own polymerases to replicate which leads to this higher rate of mutation
  • DNA viruses use host cell machinery
  • It is an advantageous characteristic, as the viruses can evolve quicker.

What other distinction is there between DNA and RNA viruses?

  • DNA viruses are much larger than RNA viruses
  • RNA viruses are around 30 kb pairs big, DNA viruses are about 100kb pairs big.

What is negative sense RNA?

  • This is RNA derived from the complimentary strand to the mRNA strand (so tRNA)
  • Viruses that carry this form of RNA have to convert the tRNA into mRNA, which can then be translated

What is the role of an integrase enzyme in the viral replication cycle?

Integrases are used to incorporate the viral DNA into the host cell DNA genome, so that it can be transcribed together and produce the viral proteins.

What is the negative sense strand in RNA?

  • The strand that is complementary to mRNA
  • mRNA can be synthesised using the negative sense strand.

What is the cytopathic effect?

The death of a host cell caused by a virus when it replicates and causes lysis/apoptosis of the host cell.

What is a plaque?

  • It is a hole in a layer of cells that is caused by the action of a virus when it kills a host cell
  • The hole is present due to the absence of the cell that was previously in that position.

What is the plaque assay used for?

  • To quantify infectious virus by counting plaques (clear zones) that form where virus has lysed a monolayer of cultured cells.
  • Each plaque is taken as arising from one infectious particle (PFU — plaque-forming unit).

Standard virology assay; lecture 4.10 stem.

What is a syncitium?

  • A fusion of cells together
  • Viruses with surface proteins can usually form syncitia at neutral pH.

What are the five ways that the presence of a virus can be detected diagnostically? Viruses are small. They range in size from…?

1 nanometer to 10 micrometers

Recall two RNA viruses.

  • HIV
  • SARS-CoV-2
  • Zika virus
  • Hepatitis C

What is the therapeutic index in the context of making treatments against viruses?

  • Therapeutic index ≈ ratio of toxic dose to effective dose (how selective the drug is for virus vs host).
  • Antivirals need a favourable therapeutic index because viruses use host machinery — the drug should inhibit viral processes much more than essential host processes.

Standard pharmacology; lecture 4.10.

What is a substrate analogue?

  • A molecule that resembles the natural substrate of an enzyme and binds its active site, often inhibiting the reaction or being incorporated incorrectly.

Standard enzymology / antiviral design.

What is a nucleoside analogue?

  • A drug that mimics a natural nucleoside (sugar + base).
  • After phosphorylation to a nucleotide form inside cells, it can inhibit viral polymerases and/or cause chain termination when incorporated into viral nucleic acid.
  • Examples: acyclovir, remdesivir, many NRTIs.

NCBI / standard antiviral pharmacology; lecture 4.10.

What is a Directly Acting Antiviral?

  • A drug that targets a viral enzyme or protein directly (not via host immune modulation).
  • Classic example class: hepatitis C DAAs targeting viral protease, polymerase, or NS5A.

Standard HCV therapy terminology.

What is acyclovir?

  • A guanosine nucleoside analogue used especially against herpesviruses (HSV, VZV).
  • It requires initial phosphorylation by viral thymidine kinase, then host kinases → acyclo-GTP, which inhibits viral DNA polymerase / causes chain termination.

NCBI StatPearls / standard antiviral teaching; lecture 4.10.

Describe the structure of acyclovir.

  • Guanosine nucleoside analogue with an acyclic side chain instead of a complete sugar ring.
  • After phosphorylation it mimics a nucleotide and can be used by viral DNA polymerase.

Standard acyclovir chemistry; lecture 4.10.

How does acyclovir’s structure allow it to work? Which viruses? How can resistance arise?

  • As a nucleoside analogue, activated acyclovir triphosphate competes with dGTP and causes chain termination when incorporated into viral DNA (lacks the 3′-OH needed for extension).
  • Used mainly against herpesviruses — especially HSV and VZV.
  • Resistance commonly via mutations reducing viral thymidine kinase activity (poor activation) or altering viral DNA polymerase.

NCBI StatPearls / standard herpesvirus antiviral teaching; lecture 4.10.

How is acyclovir initially phosphorylated to form acyclo-GTP?

  • First phosphorylation is preferentially by viral thymidine kinase → acyclovir monophosphate.
  • Host cellular kinases then add the second and third phosphates → acyclovir triphosphate (acyclo-GTP).

Standard acyclovir activation pathway; lecture 4.10.

What two factors spare cellular DNA from the most potent effects of acyclovir?

  • Preferential initial phosphorylation by viral (not host) thymidine kinase → much higher activation in infected cells.
  • Viral DNA polymerase incorporates acyclo-GTP more readily / is more sensitive than host DNA polymerases.

Standard selective toxicity of acyclovir; lecture 4.10.

How does the action of Remdesivir differ to that of acyclovir?

  • Both are nucleoside analogues that interfere with viral nucleic acid synthesis.
  • Acyclovir targets herpesvirus DNA polymerase (DNA viruses).
  • Remdesivir (as its triphosphate) targets viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase of RNA viruses such as SARS-CoV-2 (and was studied for other RNA viruses).

Standard antiviral mechanisms; lecture 4.10.

What is Remdesivir used for?

  • Remdesivir is an adenosine nucleoside analogue prodrug used for COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2); it inhibits viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase after conversion to its active triphosphate.

FDA/NHS-aligned indication summary; lecture 4.10.

Recall four nucleoside analogues.

  • Acyclovir (and related: valaciclovir / ganciclovir)
  • Remdesivir
  • HIV NRTIs (e.g. zidovudine, tenofovir, emtricitabine, lamivudine)
  • Other examples: sofosbuvir (HCV), entecavir (HBV)

Lecture 4.10 + standard antiviral examples.

What is amantadine and what specifically is it effective against?

  • An M2 ion-channel inhibitor used historically against influenza A.
  • Not effective against influenza B (which lacks the same M2 target).

CDC/NCBI influenza antiviral teaching; lecture 4.10.

Describe how amantadine works.

  • Blocks the influenza A M2 proton channel.
  • Prevents acidification of the viral interior needed for uncoating / release of viral ribonucleoprotein into the host cell.

Standard influenza A M2 inhibitor mechanism; lecture 4.10.

What is a limitation of amantadine that has led to increased viral resistance?

  • Single-point mutations in M2 readily confer resistance.
  • Widespread resistance in circulating influenza A has made adamantanes generally no longer recommended.

CDC influenza antiviral resistance guidance; lecture 4.10.

How do neuraminidase inhibitors work?

  • They block influenza neuraminidase, which cleaves sialic acid to release new virions from the infected cell surface.
  • Inhibition traps newly formed virions, limiting spread within the respiratory tract.

Standard influenza NA inhibitor mechanism; lecture 4.10.

Recall two neuraminidase inhibitors.

  • Oseltamivir (Tamiflu) — oral
  • Zanamivir (Relenza) — inhaled
  • (Also: peramivir IV in some formularies)

Lecture 4.10 / standard influenza antivirals.

What side effects can neuraminidase inhibitors have?

  • Oseltamivir: nausea/vomiting are common; neuropsychiatric effects reported rarely (especially in children/adolescents — monitor).
  • Zanamivir: bronchospasm risk — caution in asthma/COPD; not for patients who cannot inhale effectively.

BNF/CDC-aligned adverse-effect summary; lecture 4.10.

Describe the influenza mutation that renders oseltamivir (Tamiflu) ineffective.

  • Classic resistance mutation: neuraminidase H275Y (H274Y in N2 numbering) reduces oseltamivir binding/efficacy.
  • Some NA mutations affect oseltamivir more than zanamivir.

Influenza NA resistance literature / CDC; lecture 4.10.

What drug was developed / used when Tamiflu resistance was a problem?

  • Zanamivir (Relenza) remains active against many oseltamivir-resistant strains.
  • Later agents include peramivir and baloxavir (different mechanism — cap-dependent endonuclease inhibitor).

Lecture 4.10 stem + standard influenza antiviral options.

Why was Relenza not such a successful drug as Tamiflu?

  • It is inhaled (diskhaler), which is less convenient than oral oseltamivir.
  • Harder to use in young children / some elderly patients; contraindicated or cautioned in significant airways disease.

Standard clinical pharmacology comparison; lecture 4.10.

How does Baloxavir work?

  • Baloxavir marboxil is a cap-dependent endonuclease inhibitor (targets the PA subunit of the influenza polymerase complex).
  • Blocks viral mRNA synthesis (“cap snatching”), stopping replication.

FDA/NCBI influenza polymerase inhibitor teaching; lecture 4.10.

Why can Baloxavir lose effectiveness?

  • Resistance mutations in the polymerase PA endonuclease region (e.g. I38T) can emerge, sometimes even after a single dose.
  • Resistant variants may reduce clinical usefulness in some settings / viral subtypes.

Influenza baloxavir resistance reports; lecture 4.10.

What does hepatitis C cause? What do HCV and HIV drugs usually target?

  • HCV causes chronic hepatitis that can progress to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma.
  • Modern HCV DAAs typically target viral proteins such as NS3/4A protease, NS5B polymerase, and NS5A.
  • HIV drugs commonly target reverse transcriptase (NRTI/NNRTI), protease, integrase, and entry (e.g. CCR5 / fusion inhibitors).

Standard HCV/HIV antiviral targets; lecture 4.10.

How did Timothy Brown (“the Berlin patient”) become cured of HIV?

  • He received allogeneic stem-cell transplants for leukaemia from a donor homozygous for the CCR5-Δ32 mutation.
  • Without functional CCR5 co-receptor, R5-tropic HIV struggles to infect new cells — leading to sustained remission/cure after transplant.
  • This is not a routine therapy (high risk), but proved a conceptual cure pathway.

Landmark HIV cure case reports; lecture 4.10.

What is a “biological”?

  • A medicine derived from living systems / biological sources — typically large molecules such as monoclonal antibodies, recombinant proteins, or antibody cocktails.
  • Lecture example: Regeneron antibody cocktail used against COVID-19.

Lecture 4.10 (Regeneron example) + standard biologics definition.

What are the two receptors used by HIV to enter host cells?

  • CD4 receptors
  • CCR5 / CXCR4 (co-receptors)

What is regeneron?

A “biological” against COVID-19

What is PrEP?

Pre-Exposure Prohpylaxis is a treatment for HIV administered to patients before they are exposed to HIV.

What effect does Dexamethasone have on hosts?

  • Decrease the inflammatory response.
  • It is a steroid
  • Sometimes in severely ill patients, the inflammatory response is causing damage to the host, and so immunosuppressors such as dexamethasone can be used to control this response and help the patient

What is the difference between an NRTI and an NNRTI?

  • NRTI (nucleoside/nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitor): substrate analogue that competes with natural nucleotides and often causes chain termination.
  • NNRTI (non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor): binds an allosteric site on HIV reverse transcriptase and inhibits it without mimicking nucleotides.

Standard HIV pharmacology; lecture 4.10.

What is the pK of a drug?

The pharmacokinetics (i.e how the body interacts with the drug)

Why is it important to consider the pharmacokinetics of a drug when using two or more in treatment?

  • Drugs interact via absorption, metabolism (e.g. CYP), distribution or excretion.
  • One drug can raise/lower another’s level → toxicity or treatment failure.
  • Combination regimens (HIV/HCV) must keep each agent in its therapeutic window.

Standard clinical PK / ARV combination teaching; lecture 4.10.

How many antiretroviral drugs are usually given to treat HIV and why?

  • 3+
  • This is because we want to reduce the rate at which mutations can be selected for within the viral population NOTE: Diagnosis (i.e which virus the patient is infected with) is very important when considering which anti-viral agent to we need to use. This is because of the specificity of these agents that currently exists.
