Overview

What does HTLV-1 cause?

  • HTLV-1 can cause adult T cell leukaemia (cancer)
  • It was the first virus to be found to cause malignancy
  • Approximately 5% of infected patients will develop cancer

Why is diagnosis of HTLV-1 infections sometimes difficult?

  • HTLV-1 can cause many different diseases with different symptoms, so it can be difficult to identify that HTLV-1 is responsible for symptoms seen in a patient
  • These diseases include:
  • Adult T-cell leukaemia
  • Adult T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma
  • HTLV-1-associated myelopathy
  • Tropical spastic paraparesis (TSP)
  • HTLV-1-associated infectious dermatitis
  • HTLV-1-associated uveitis

How can HTLV-1 be transmitted?

  • Mother to infant during pregnancy
  • Through sexual intercourse
  • Blood-to-blood contact (i.e transfusions/sharing needles)

Is HTLV-1 a single or double-stranded virus?

HTLV-1 is a single-stranded RNA virus

What is Tax?

  • Tax is a gene that is essential for coding for proteins useful in transcription and viral replication
  • Tax is also known to have severe oncogenic potential by accelerating the transition from the G1 to S phase as well as causing mutations which can lead to cancer.

How can Tax be used diagnostically? Which type of cells does HTLV-1 show preferential infection for?

  • Tax is an HTLV-1 regulatory oncoprotein; detecting tax sequences/expression can support molecular evidence of infected cells / burden.
  • HTLV-1 preferentially infects CD4⁺ T lymphocytes.

HTLV-1 biology; lecture 4.11.

Describe the replication cycle of HTLV-1 from infection.

  • Entry into target cells (CD4⁺ T cells) via viral envelope–receptor interactions.
  • Reverse transcription of viral RNA → DNA; integration into the host genome as a provirus (hallmark of retroviruses).
  • Viral gene expression (including tax/rex) using host transcription machinery.
  • Assembly and budding of new virions; persistence often maintained by proliferation of infected clones as well as new infection events.

Standard HTLV-1/retrovirus replication teaching; lecture 4.11.

Why is HTLV-1 easily spread through blood transfusions?

  • The virus is highly cell-associated (lives in infected lymphocytes).
  • Transfusion of cellular blood products can transfer infected cells efficiently.
  • This is why blood screening for HTLV is used in many transfusion services.

Transfusion-transmitted HTLV teaching; lecture 4.11.

What is the purpose of quantitative real-time PCR?

  • To amplify and quantify a DNA (or cDNA) target in real time.
  • In HTLV-1 work, used to estimate proviral load (how much viral DNA is present in the sample).

Standard qPCR teaching; lecture 4.11.

What is meant by “clonal proliferation is the dominant contributor…”?

  • Once integrated, HTLV-1-infected T-cell clones can expand by ordinary mitosis, copying the provirus into daughter cells.
  • In chronic infection, growth of existing infected clones often increases infected-cell numbers more than brand-new infections of previously uninfected cells.

HTLV-1 persistence biology; lecture 4.11.

What are peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs)?

  • Blood cells with a round nucleus: mainly lymphocytes and monocytes.
  • Separated from granulocytes/RBCs for many immunology and viral PCR assays.

Standard haematology/immunology definition; lecture 4.11.

How is Ficoll gradient centrifugation important? In which direction is DNA synthesised in PCR?

  • Ficoll density-gradient centrifugation isolates PBMCs (where HTLV-1 provirus resides) from whole blood before DNA extraction/PCR.
  • DNA is synthesised 5′ → 3′.

Standard lab methods + PCR polarity; lecture 4.11.

Describe the three steps involved in the PCR.

  • Denaturation — heat separates DNA strands.
  • Annealing — primers bind complementary sequences at a lower temperature.
  • Extension/elongation — thermostable polymerase extends primers, copying the target.

Standard PCR teaching; lecture 4.11.

How many times are these PCR steps repeated?

  • Typically ~25–40 cycles (often ~30–35 in diagnostic protocols), exponentially amplifying the target.

Standard PCR cycling; lecture 4.11.

What is the purpose of the forward and reverse primers in PCR?

  • They define the exact region to be amplified by binding opposite strands at either end of the target.
  • Polymerase extends from each primer, producing a defined-length amplicon.

Standard PCR teaching; lecture 4.11.

What is the purpose of the buffer solution in the PCR reaction?

  • Maintains optimal pH and ionic conditions (often with Mg²⁺) for polymerase activity and primer annealing.

Standard PCR reagents; lecture 4.11.

What is Taq polymerase and why is it used?

  • A thermostable DNA polymerase from Thermus aquaticus.
  • It withstands repeated high-temperature denaturation steps, so enzyme need not be replenished each cycle.

Standard PCR teaching; lecture 4.11.

What technique is used to visualise the DNA produced from PCR?

  • Agarose gel electrophoresis (with DNA stain), or fluorescence readout in real-time PCR without a gel.

Standard molecular methods; lecture 4.11.

How do we know amplicon size is ~300 bp? Which stains visualise DNA on gels?

  • Compare band migration to a DNA ladder/marker of known sizes on the same gel.
  • Common stains: ethidium bromide; safer dyes such as SYBR Safe / GelRed (lecture may list two course-preferred stains).

Standard gel electrophoresis teaching; lecture 4.11.

When can PCR results automatically be assumed invalid?

  • If negative/positive controls fail (e.g. no amplification of a housekeeping gene; contamination in no-template control).
  • Also if extraction blank is positive, or expected internal control is absent.

Standard molecular QC; lecture 4.11.

What are the five essential components required for PCR mixtures?

  • Template DNA
  • Forward and reverse primers
  • Thermostable DNA polymerase (e.g. Taq)
  • dNTPs (nucleotides)
  • Buffer (with appropriate Mg²⁺ / salts)

Standard PCR components; lecture 4.11.

What are the names of the primers used in PCR for HTLV-1?

  • The exact primer pair is assay-specific (designed against conserved HTLV-1 regions such as tax or LTR).
  • Classic published diagnostic pairs include SK43/SK44 (tax region) in older literature; modern labs use validated kit primers.
  • Your Imperial practical likely names a specific pair — check the lab script if a named pair was given.

What is nested PCR?

  • Two successive PCRs: a first (outer) amplification, then a second reaction using primers inside the first amplicon.
  • Increases sensitivity and specificity for low-copy targets.

Standard nested PCR teaching; lecture 4.11.

How can Western blotting detect anti-HTLV-1 antibodies? Which viral proteins?

  • HTLV-1 proteins are separated by electrophoresis, transferred to a membrane, then incubated with patient serum.
  • Bound patient antibodies are detected with labelled anti-human Ig — bands indicate reactivity to specific viral antigens (confirmatory testing after ELISA screening).
  • Antibodies commonly recognise Gag proteins (e.g. p19/p24) and Env glycoproteins (e.g. gp46/gp21); Tax antibodies may also appear.

HTLV-1 serology teaching; lecture 4.11.

What is the purpose of quantitative real-time PCR (qrt-PCR)?

  • Quantify target nucleic acid as amplification proceeds using fluorescence.
  • For HTLV-1: estimate proviral load in PBMCs.

Standard qPCR; lecture 4.11.

How does the fluorescent dye in qPCR work?

  • Intercalating dyes (e.g. SYBR Green) bind double-stranded DNA and fluoresce more strongly as amplicon accumulates each cycle.
  • Fluorescence is measured every cycle to plot an amplification curve.

Standard dye-based qPCR; lecture 4.11.

What is the cycle threshold (CT) in qPCR?

  • The cycle number at which fluorescence exceeds a set threshold above background.
  • Lower CT ≈ more starting template (detectable sooner).

Standard qPCR CT definition; lecture 4.11.

What is the Taqman method in qrt-PCR?

  • It is an alternative method used instead of the fluorescent dye PCR method which requires the use of an oligo to bind onto the amplifying region before amplification begins
  • The oligo consists of both a fluorescent region and a quencher region and will be cleaved apart as the DNA polymerase moves along the chain
  • This means the level of fluorescence seen per cycle will be proportional to the levels of template DNA present at each point
  • i.e the more of the Taq template DNA present in the sample (i.e higher HTLV-1 load), the more oligos will be cleaved by DNA polymerase and hence the more fluorescence will be reported
  • This increases the specificity of the reaction, and although it is more expensive, this method (Taqman method) is what is commonly used for diagnostic purposes

Describe how qrt-PCR is used to determine the viral load of a patient. NOTE: clarify “quantitative real-time” vs “quantitative reverse-transcriptase” PCR nomenclature.

  • Extract nucleic acid from patient cells/plasma and run quantitative PCR against a standard curve of known copy number.
  • Convert the patient CT value to copy number (e.g. HTLV-1 copies per number of PBMCs).
  • Nomenclature caution: qrt-PCR may mean quantitative real-time PCR (DNA) or quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RNA).

Standard viral-load quantification; lecture 4.11.
